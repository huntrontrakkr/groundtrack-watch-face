Home station

Home is drawn as an airport, one more station of the network. Over the chart it gives the day’s sunrise and sunset there (moonrise and moonset for the Moon), in local 24-hour time. For a satellite it gives the next pass: AOS, the minute it rises 10° above the home horizon, how long it stays up and its highest elevation. On the world views the home acquisition circle shows where the satellite must be to be seen. My location asks the browser once and keeps the place in this page only.

Events

    An event is a compulsory reporting point: a filled triangle on the route at its minute, named above with a five-letter name code made from its title, as airspace names its waypoints (Standup is STAND, Dinner is DINNR, Run is RUNNN). Codes are pronounceable and unique on the day. The next hour’s open triangle is the optional kind. On a watch, events would come from the phone’s timeline; here they are kept in the page only.

    Moonlight

    The same Moon over three evenings. On the paper plates, night is a printed dot tint that deepens through civil twilight.

    Projection

    A spike: the icosahedron rolls along the route, printing each face as it touches down, and floats as a net on the paper: the route’s faces and their true neighbours, with no cuts. Time runs left to right; the compass rose shows where north is. The Sun and Moon show their whole local day as one rolled strip, with a time callout over it.

    Plate
    Read the chart

    The hour figures stand over their stations: this hour solid over its compass rose, the next outlined over its reporting point. Between them the magenta route is a graduated scale, two pixels a minute, with longer marks at five and fifteen minutes and the quarters numbered. The Sun or Moon symbol is the index; the bold line behind it is the hour already flown. On the ISS world view the same scale is set in a panel above the map, with a triangular index. Under Clock, a time callout can give the time in full, hung from a leader to the body. Filled triangles on the route are your events, at their minute. Contours fall every 1,000 m, dotted at 500 m; offshore, the dotted blue line is the 200 m edge of the continental shelf. Three-letter codes are the tracking stations of NASA’s Mercury and Apollo networks. The margin carries the date, the time in Zulu (UTC, as pilots keep it) and the day of the year.

    THE CHART

    Real ground, lightly drawn.

    Relief comes from NOAA’s ETOPO1 and the USGS GMTED2010 elevation models, generalized and contoured like a jet navigation chart. The continental shelf edge is the real 200 m depth line. Small crosses mark every five degrees, and degree ticks run around the edge like a neatline.

    THE ROUTE

    Two fixes and a magenta line.

    This hour is a VOR in its compass rose; the next is a reporting point. The route is the magenta line pilots follow on every modern display. The route itself is graduated like a ruler, a mark every minute between the two fixes; the body on it is the index.

    THE NETWORK

    Where the astronauts were heard.

    Codes like TAN, CRO and GDS mark stations of the Mercury and Apollo tracking networks. For the ISS, each carries its acquisition circle, the reach of a low orbit, as drawn on the world maps of the first mission control rooms.

    About this working study

    This is a browser design study, not an installable watchface. Every watch pixel is one of the display’s 64 RGB222 colors. The map is north-up and equidistant cylindrical; for the Sun and Moon the hour’s two stations sit 120 pixels apart, directly under the time scale’s hour marks, and the ISS, which travels about 240° of longitude in an hour, is shown on a world band. Scenes are frozen until you interact: there is no animation loop, polling or sensor use, and the page asks for your location only if you choose My location as home.

    Coastlines are Natural Earth 1:50m land. Relief is a quarter-degree grid built from the AWS Open Data Terrain Tiles at zoom 3, whose sources there are NOAA ETOPO1 and USGS GMTED2010, both public domain; heights are smoothed before contouring and are not for navigation. Day, civil twilight and night come from the calculated Sun at the selected minute. Tracking-station positions are approximate, from public histories of the networks. Acquisition circles show where a 410 km orbit rises five degrees above each station’s horizon, about 15.6° of arc.

    Sun and Moon positions come from Astronomy Engine. The “ISS archive” view uses a bounded June 5, 2019 element set from Satellite.js. Live satellites use current element sets fetched from CelesTrak by this page (at most once every two hours per satellite, and only within three days of their epoch) and propagated with SGP4. Figures are Jost, after Futura; chart lettering is Departure Mono by Helena Zhang; headlines are Michroma, after Microgramma. All three are SIL OFL 1.1. Sources and credits.
